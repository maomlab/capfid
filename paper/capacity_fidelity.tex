\title{\vspace{-1.2em}\Large\bf How Many Bits Does a Training Example Cost?\\
Per-Example Capacity versus Fidelity via Linearized Laplace Unlearning}

\begin{abstract}
The compression/fidelity trade-off is usually stated for a whole model: parameters are a code whose length is traded against the code length of the data given those parameters. We ask the per-example version of the question: how much of a trained network's capacity is spent on each training example (or set of examples), and how well is that example fit? We define capacity by \emph{unlearning}: the change in a linearized Laplace posterior over the weights when an example is removed, measured as (i) the effective number of parameters it determines, (ii) the drop in posterior differential entropy (a label-agnostic \emph{volume}), and (iii) the Kullback--Leibler divergence between the posteriors with and without it (the label-dependent \emph{information} actually stored). All three reduce to $C\times C$ output-space matrices, so every example is scored after a single factorization, and arbitrary sets are scored jointly. Under exact Bayesian updating, information capacity equals the example's generalization gap (training minus held-out log-likelihood); volume equals the mutual information between weights and label for Gaussian likelihoods; and volume is submodular, which yields a redundancy ratio for sets. In a closed-form linear model all identities hold to $10^{-13}$ bits. On a two-moons MLP with 10\% flipped labels, sweeping the prior precision moves flipped examples from \emph{ignored} (low fidelity, $\sim$0.08 bits each) to \emph{memorized} (high fidelity, $\sim$1.6 bits each), while the Laplace evidence favors the most compressed model. On a 4{,}130-parameter vision transformer trained on $8\times 8$ digits with 5\% flipped labels, flipped examples cost a median of 3.5 bits versus 0.05 bits for clean ones, 14 of the 16 most expensive images are flips, and capacity rank-correlates with memorization measured by actual retraining (Spearman 0.84--0.88). The one-step held-out prediction, however, overestimates the effect of removing heavily memorized examples by up to several-fold, which we document as the main limitation. Computing the posterior in output space through the empirical neural tangent kernel removes the dependence on the number of parameters, which lets us analyze 18 molecular property-prediction networks of up to 4.2 million parameters: capacity ranks the compounds those networks memorize, as measured by 864 retrainings (Spearman 0.38--0.73), and the compounds it flags are label conflicts with structural alerts, ionizable compounds whose $\log D$ the representations cannot express, inorganic salts, and groups of compounds that a fingerprint maps to one vector but whose labels span six log units.\ifanonymous\else{} Code: \coderepo.\fi
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Minimum description length (MDL) and the information bottleneck frame learning as a trade-off between how much information a model stores and how faithfully it reproduces data \citep{rissanen1978,hinton1993,tishby2000,grunwald2007}. A model with a short parameter code that explains the data well generalizes; a model that stores many bits to fit a few examples memorizes. These statements are global. In practice one often wants the \emph{per-example} decomposition: which examples consume capacity, how much, and what the model gets in return in fidelity. This matters for auditing memorization \citep{zhang2017,feldman2020,feldman2020neural}, diagnosing label noise, identifying long-tail subpopulations, and valuing data \citep{ghorbani2019,pruthi2020,covert2024}, a question economists study as the value of information \citep{raiffa1961,abadie2023}.

Two families of tools approximate pieces of this picture. Influence functions estimate how parameters or predictions change when an example is removed \citep{cook1977,koh2017}, and memorization scores compare held-in and held-out likelihood, typically by retraining many models \citep{feldman2020neural}. Both ask what changes when an example is withheld, but they answer in incommensurable units, a parameter shift or a gap in log-likelihood, neither of which says what the example cost the model. Information-theoretic work supplies a common currency for that comparison: the information an example leaves in the weights, defined as the divergence between weight distributions trained with and without it \citep{achille2019,harutyunyan2021}, and measured in bits like the description lengths above. We build on this line and make three things explicit: a family of capacity measures with distinct meanings, identities that tie them to fidelity and to classical quantities, and a two-dimensional \emph{capacity--fidelity} diagnostic that can be computed for every example of a small network in seconds.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item We define three nested per-example (and per-set) capacity measures by Laplace unlearning: effective degrees of freedom, volume (entropy drop), and information (KL), and show they reduce exactly to $C\times C$ output-space quantities (\cref{prop:reduction}).
\item We prove or collect identities that give them meaning: information equals the generalization gap under exact Bayesian updating (\cref{prop:bayes}); volume equals the weight--label mutual information for Gaussian likelihoods and the leverage formula $-\tfrac12\log(1-h_{ii})$ in linear regression; degrees of freedom is exactly additive, so per-example values sum to MacKay's effective number of parameters $\gamma$ (\cref{rem:totals}); and volume is submodular, giving a redundancy ratio $\ge 1$ for sets and an upper bound on the fraction of model information attributable to single examples (\cref{prop:submod}).
\item We connect the totals to a Laplace two-part MDL code (\cref{sec:mdl}) and demonstrate the diagnostic on an exactly solvable model, an MLP under a prior-precision sweep, and a small vision transformer, validating against warm-started retraining and documenting where the approximation fails.
\item We compute the same posterior in output space through the empirical neural tangent kernel, at a cost independent of the number of parameters, and refit its mean for networks that are not at a stationary point, such as those trained with dropout or early stopping (\cref{sec:kernel}). This makes the analysis practical for networks with millions of parameters and a few thousand training examples, which we use for a case study of molecular property prediction (\cref{sec:tdc}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and related work}
\paragraph{Laplace approximations.} The Laplace approximation fits a Gaussian at a mode using the curvature of the negative log posterior \citep{mackay1992}. For neural networks the generalized Gauss--Newton (GGN) curvature is positive semidefinite and corresponds to a linearization of the network in its parameters \citep{ritter2018,immer2021,daxberger2021}. The Laplace marginal likelihood penalizes models by an Occam factor that MacKay related to the effective number of well-determined parameters, $\gamma$.

\paragraph{Leverage and influence.} In linear regression the diagonal $h_{ii}$ of the hat matrix measures how much an observation determines its own fit, and $\sum_i h_{ii}$ is the model's degrees of freedom \citep{hoaglin1978,cook1977}. Influence functions generalize the parameter shift to differentiable models \citep{koh2017}, but are known to be fragile for deep networks, especially for highly influential points \citep{basu2021,bae2022}.

\paragraph{Information in the weights and generalization.} \citet{achille2019} define the information a dataset stores in the weights as a KL to a prior, and \citet{harutyunyan2021} introduced \emph{smooth unique information}: a KL between weight distributions trained with and without an example, computed with a linearized network. Information-theoretic generalization bounds control the gap between train and test loss by the mutual information between weights and individual examples \citep{xu2017,bu2020}. Our \cref{prop:bayes} is the pointwise, exact-posterior version of this connection. MDL views of deep learning \citep{hinton1993,blier2018} motivate reading the Laplace evidence as a two-part code.

\paragraph{Value of information and the economics of data.} Statistical decision theory values data by what it does for decisions: the expected value of sample information is the expected improvement in the payoff of the best action after observing data, net of the cost of collecting it \citep{blackwell1951,raiffa1961,howard1966}. \citet{abadie2023} turned this into an empirical-Bayes estimator of the value of evidence-based decision making. A decision maker with prior $\tau\sim\N(\mu,\gamma^2)$ on a policy's payoff can buy a signal $\hat\tau\sim\N(\tau,\sigma^2)$ and launch only if the posterior mean is positive; the value of evidence is the resulting gain in expected payoff minus its cost, estimated from many past experiments. They show how this value changes with statistical precision and that launch rules based on statistical significance can leave it unrealized. Two connections to our measures are exact. First, when the utility is the logarithmic score of a reported belief about the parameters, the expected value of an experiment is its expected KL divergence from prior to posterior, that is, the mutual information between parameters and data \citep{lindley1956,bernardo1979}. By \cref{cor:mi}, our volume $\Vol(S)$ is exactly this quantity for the labels of $S$ given the rest of the data (for Gaussian likelihoods): it is the \emph{ex ante} value of those labels to such a decision maker, and $\Inf(S)$ is their \emph{ex post} value once observed. Second, in their Gaussian model the value of evidence depends on precision only through the shrinkage weight $h=\gamma^2/(\gamma^2+\sigma^2)$, which is the single-observation leverage $K$ of \cref{prop:reduction}; the corresponding volume is $-\tfrac12\log(1-h)=\tfrac12\log(1+\gamma^2/\sigma^2)$. The frameworks differ in what is valued. Theirs prices evidence by a downstream decision and its cost; ours prices training examples in model capacity and fit, with no decision layer. Selecting $\alpha$ by maximizing the Laplace evidence (\cref{sec:mdl}), which we report but did not use to set $\alpha$, would be the same empirical-Bayes step they use to estimate the prior from a population of studies.

\paragraph{Data valuation, scaling laws, and large language models.} Data valuation in machine learning assigns each example a share of held-out performance, for example through Shapley values \citep{ghorbani2019} or gradient tracing \citep{pruthi2020}. \citet{covert2024} found that an example's marginal contribution to performance shrinks log-linearly with the size of the dataset it joins, with example-specific exponents: some examples matter most in small datasets and others remain useful in large ones. Our capacity is an information-theoretic counterpart of their marginal contribution, evaluated at the full dataset (a subset of size $N-1$) and measured as information stored rather than held-out loss. With curvatures held at $\theta^*$, \cref{prop:submod} implies that an example's marginal volume can only shrink as the dataset it joins grows, the diminishing returns their scaling laws quantify, and the Laplace approximation gives this marginal volume in closed form for any subset, so information-scaling curves could be traced without retraining. At the largest scale, influence functions with eigenvalue-corrected Kronecker-factored curvature have been applied to language models with up to 52 billion parameters to trace behaviors back to pretraining documents \citep{grosse2023}. The same curvature approximations are the natural route to scaling our capacity measures (\cref{sec:compute}).

\paragraph{Local surrogates and feature attribution.} A large explainability literature asks what a prediction depends on rather than what a training example costs. LIME explains one prediction by fitting an interpretable surrogate, typically a sparse linear model, to the black box in a neighborhood of the input, on the premise that a model too complicated to describe globally is simple enough to describe locally \citep{ribeiro2016}. We adopt the same premise in weight space rather than input space: \cref{eq:laplace} replaces the network by a surrogate that is linear in $\theta$ near $\theta^*$, and every quantity we report is read off that surrogate. The two differ in what the surrogate is local to and what it yields. LIME's is local to one input, fit by sampling perturbations of it, and yields a feature attribution; ours is local to one parameter vector, obtained from the curvature of the training objective, and yields a posterior, which is what lets us delete data from it in closed form and report the result in bits. SHAP attributes a prediction to features by the Shapley value of a cooperative game among them, whose payoff is the model's expected output, or its performance in loss-based variants, when a coalition of features is supplied and the rest are withheld \citep{lundberg2017}; the same game played over training examples rather than features is Data Shapley \citep{ghorbani2019}. In contrast to SHAP, we hold the features fixed and withhold data; in contrast to Data Shapley, we report two quantities in place of a single payoff: the capacity a set of examples occupies and the fidelity with which it is fit, so that an example a model fits for free and one it fits by spending capacity are distinguished rather than summarized by one number. We also evaluate a single coalition, the full training set, instead of averaging over all of them. That is why a score costs one posterior and no refitting, and also why it is conservative: by \cref{prop:submod} leave-one-out marginals undercount the total, and the Shapley-style average over removal orders that would distribute $\Vol_{\mathrm{tot}}$ exactly remains an extension.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Setup}
Let $\D=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$, $f(x;\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^C$ a network with $\theta\in\mathbb{R}^P$, and $\ell(y,f)=-\log p(y\mid f)$. We train a MAP estimate $\theta^*$ of
\begin{equation}
L(\theta)=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^N \ell\big(y_i,f(x_i;\theta)\big)+\tfrac{\alpha}{2}\|\theta\|^2,
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
i.e., a summed loss with a Gaussian prior $\N(0,\alpha^{-1}I)$. At $\theta^*$ define the Jacobian $J_i=\partial f(x_i)/\partial\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times P}$, the output-space residual $r_i=\nabla_f\ell\in\mathbb{R}^C$, and curvature $\Lambda_i=\nabla^2_f\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times C}$ (for softmax, $r_i=p_i-e_{y_i}$ and $\Lambda_i=\mathrm{diag}(p_i)-p_ip_i\T$). The linearized Laplace posterior is
\begin{equation}
q_\D=\N(\theta^*,\Sigma),\qquad \Sigma^{-1}=A=\textstyle\sum_i J_i\T\Lambda_iJ_i+\alpha I.
\label{eq:laplace}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Unlearning a set.} For $S\subseteq[N]$ with $m=|S|$, stack $J_S\in\mathbb{R}^{mC\times P}$, $r_S\in\mathbb{R}^{mC}$, and $\Lambda_S=\mathrm{blkdiag}(\Lambda_i)_{i\in S}$. Removing $S$ subtracts its curvature and, because $\nabla L(\theta^*)=0$, one Newton step on $L-\sum_{i\in S}\ell_i$ gives
\begin{equation}
A_{-S}=A-J_S\T\Lambda_SJ_S,\qquad \theta^*_{-S}=\theta^*+A_{-S}^{-1}J_S\T r_S,
\label{eq:unlearn}
\end{equation}
and $q_{\DS}=\N(\theta^*_{-S},A_{-S}^{-1})$. This is exact for linear-Gaussian models.

\subsection{Capacity and fidelity}
\begin{definition}[Capacity]\label{def:capacity}
For a set $S$,
\begin{align*}
\dof(S)&=\tr\!\big(\Sigma\,J_S\T\Lambda_SJ_S\big) &&\text{(degrees of freedom)}\\
\Vol(S)&=h(q_{\DS})-h(q_\D) &&\text{(volume)}\\
\Inf(S)&=\KL\big(q_\D\,\|\,q_{\DS}\big) &&\text{(information)}
\end{align*}
with $h$ the differential entropy. We report $\Vol$ and $\Inf$ in bits.
\end{definition}
$\Vol$ is the log-ratio of weight-space volumes the examples pin down; it depends on the inputs and on the rest of the data, but not on the labels $y_S$ except through $\Lambda_S$. $\Inf$ adds a mean-shift term and measures what is actually stored about the labeled examples. Unlike an absolute differential entropy, $\Vol$ is invariant to affine reparameterizations of $\theta$ (and, within the Laplace approximation, to any smooth reparameterization, which is linearized at $\theta^*$ so that the Jacobian terms cancel); $\Inf$ is invariant to any bijection.

\begin{remark}[The three totals, and $\gamma$]\label{rem:totals}
Unlearning all of $\D$ returns the prior ($A_{-\D}=\alpha I$, and $\theta^*_{-\D}=0$ because $\nabla L(\theta^*)=0$), so evaluating \cref{def:capacity} at $S=\D$ gives the three model-level totals:
\begin{equation}
\dof(\D)=\gamma,\quad \Vol(\D)=\Vol_{\mathrm{tot}},\quad \Inf(\D)=\KL(q_\D\|\mathrm{prior}),
\label{eq:totals}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma=\tr(\Sigma\,\mathrm{GGN})=P-\alpha\tr\Sigma$ is MacKay's effective number of parameters \citep{mackay1992} and $\Vol_{\mathrm{tot}}=\tfrac12\logdet(I+\mathrm{GGN}/\alpha)$ is the bits by which the data shrink the weight space. Every $\gamma$ in this paper is this total. Because it is a trace, $\dof$ is the only one of the three that is exactly additive,
\begin{equation}
\dof(S)=\textstyle\sum_{i\in S}\dof(\{i\}),\qquad \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^N\dof(\{i\})=\gamma,
\label{eq:dof-additive}
\end{equation}
so $\dof(\{i\})$ is example $i$'s exact share of the model's effective parameters. $\Vol$ is superadditive instead (\cref{prop:submod}), and $\Inf$ is not additive either, which is why sets need their own scores.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Fidelity]
The fidelity of example $i$ is $F_i=\log_2 p(y_i\mid x_i,\theta^*)$ and its held-out fidelity is $F_i^{-}=\log_2 p(y_i\mid x_i,\theta^*_{-i})$, using the linearized held-out logits of \cref{prop:reduction}. Posterior-averaged versions are $\bar F_i=\mathbb{E}_{q_\D}\log_2 p(y_i\mid\theta)$ and $\tilde F^{-}_i=\log_2\mathbb{E}_{q_{\Di}}p(y_i\mid\theta)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Output-space reduction]\label{prop:reduction}
Let $S_S=J_S\Sigma J_S\T$, $K_S=\Lambda_S^{1/2}S_S\Lambda_S^{1/2}$ and $S_{-S}=J_SA_{-S}^{-1}J_S\T=S_S+S_S\Lambda_S^{1/2}(I-K_S)^{-1}\Lambda_S^{1/2}S_S$. Then, in nats,
\begin{align*}
\dof(S)&=\tr K_S,\qquad \Vol(S)=-\tfrac12\logdet(I-K_S),\\
\Inf(S)&=\tfrac12\Big[r_S\T S_{-S}\,r_S-\tr K_S-\logdet(I-K_S)\Big],
\end{align*}
and the linearized held-out logits are $f(x_S)+S_{-S}r_S$.
\end{proposition}
All quantities involve only $mC\times mC$ matrices; for single examples they are $C\times C$. The proof (\cref{app:proofs}) uses Sylvester's determinant identity and the Woodbury identity. $K_S$ is a block of the posterior ``hat'' matrix: it is the leverage of $S$ under the Laplace posterior. Since $A_{-S}\succeq\alpha I$, the eigenvalues of $K_S$ lie in $[0,1)$.

\begin{proposition}[Information is the generalization gap]\label{prop:bayes}
If $q_\D\propto q_{\DS}\,p(y_S\mid\theta)$ exactly, then
\[
\Inf(S)=\mathbb{E}_{q_\D}\big[\log p(y_S\mid\theta)\big]-\log\mathbb{E}_{q_{\DS}}\big[p(y_S\mid\theta)\big].
\]
\end{proposition}
That is, the bits the posterior stores about $S$ equal the difference between its posterior-averaged training fit and its leave-$S$-out predictive fit. The condition holds for linear-Gaussian models, where \cref{eq:unlearn} is exact. On a plot of $\bar F_i$ against $\Inf(\{i\})$, lines of slope one are then iso-lines of held-out likelihood.

\begin{corollary}[Volume as mutual information]\label{cor:mi}
For a Gaussian likelihood, $\Sigma$ does not depend on $y$, and $\mathbb{E}_{y_S\sim p(\cdot\mid \DS)}\Inf(S)=\Vol(S)=I(\theta;y_S\mid x_S,\DS)$. For linear regression with a Gaussian prior, $\Vol(\{i\})=-\tfrac12\log(1-h_{ii})$ where $h_{ii}$ is the leverage of the Bayesian ridge hat matrix.
\end{corollary}
So $\Vol$ is the \emph{capacity allotted} to an input location (the expected information about any plausible label), while $\Inf$ is the information spent on the observed label. Expanding in the eigenvalues $\kappa$ of $K_S$: $\Vol\approx\tfrac12\sum\kappa$, whereas the covariance part of $\Inf$ is $\tfrac12\sum[-\kappa-\log(1-\kappa)]\approx\tfrac14\sum\kappa^2$. For well-fit examples (small $r$), $\Inf\approx\Vol^2$ in nats: they occupy volume but store little. Examples with surprising labels add the mean-shift term and can have $\Inf>\Vol$.

\begin{proposition}[Redundancy]\label{prop:submod}
For every $S$, $\Vol(S)\ge\sum_{i\in S}\Vol(\{i\})$, and the total model information $\Vol_{\mathrm{tot}}=h(\text{prior})-h(q_\D)=\tfrac12\logdet(I+\mathrm{GGN}/\alpha)$ satisfies $\sum_{i=1}^N\Vol(\{i\})\le\Vol_{\mathrm{tot}}$.
\end{proposition}
This follows from submodularity of $T\mapsto\logdet(\alpha I+\sum_{i\in T}J_i\T\Lambda_iJ_i)$ \citep{krause2008}. We report the \emph{redundancy ratio} $\rho(S)=\Vol(S)/\sum_{i\in S}\Vol(\{i\})\ge1$, which is large when members of $S$ encode the same information, and the \emph{unique fraction} $u=\sum_i\Vol(\{i\})/\Vol_{\mathrm{tot}}\le1$.

\subsection{Totals as a two-part code}\label{sec:mdl}
The Laplace log evidence decomposes as $-\log p(\D)\approx L_{\text{data}}+L_{\text{model}}$ with $L_{\text{data}}=-\sum_i\log p(y_i\mid x_i,\theta^*)$ and $L_{\text{model}}=\tfrac{\alpha}{2}\|\theta^*\|^2+\Vol_{\mathrm{tot}}$. Moreover the total information of \cref{rem:totals} is $\Inf(\D)=\KL(q_\D\|\text{prior})=L_{\text{model}}-\gamma/2$. Varying $\alpha$ moves along a compression/fidelity curve, and the evidence selects a point on it.

\subsection{Computation}\label{sec:compute}
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Per-example capacity and fidelity}\label{alg:main}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $J\gets$ per-example Jacobians ($N\times C\times P$; \texttt{vmap(jacrev)})
\State $A\gets\alpha I+\sum_i J_i\T\Lambda_iJ_i$;\; $L\gets\mathrm{chol}(A)$
\For{each batch $B$}
  \State $V\gets L^{-1}J_B\T$;\; $S_i\gets V_i\T V_i$ for $i\in B$
\EndFor
\For{each $i$ (batched $C\times C$ algebra)}
  \State $K_i,\ S_{-i},\ \dof,\ \Vol,\ \Inf$ via \cref{prop:reduction}
  \State $F_i$ from $f(x_i)$;\; $F_i^{-}$ from $f(x_i)+S_{-i}r_i$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\Cref{alg:main} costs $O(NCP^2+P^3)$ time and $O(P^2+NCP)$ memory for the exact GGN; set capacities need one extra triangular solve with $J_S\T$. Expectations over $q$ use Monte Carlo with common random numbers. The method is implemented in the open-source PyTorch package \texttt{capfid} (see Code availability). For larger models, only $J_i\Sigma J_i\T$ is needed, so Kronecker-factored, last-layer, or low-rank curvature can be substituted \citep{martens2015,ritter2018,daxberger2021}.

\subsection{Wide networks: computation in output space}\label{sec:kernel}
Every quantity above needs only blocks of $J\Sigma J\T$ and $\logdet(I+\mathrm{GGN}/\alpha)$. With the empirical neural tangent kernel $G=JJ\T\in\mathbb{R}^{NC\times NC}$ \citep{jacot2018}, $\Lambda=\mathrm{blkdiag}(\Lambda_i)$, and $M=\alpha I+\Lambda^{1/2}G\Lambda^{1/2}$, the Woodbury and Sylvester identities give
\begin{align}
J\Sigma J\T&=\alpha^{-1}\big[G-G\Lambda^{1/2}M^{-1}\Lambda^{1/2}G\big],\notag\\
\logdet\!\big(I+\tfrac{\mathrm{GGN}}{\alpha}\big)&=\logdet\tfrac{M}{\alpha}.
\label{eq:kernel}
\end{align}
One Cholesky factorization of $M$ therefore yields the identical posterior in $O((NC)^3)$ time, independent of $P$: the exact Laplace posterior of a network with millions of parameters is available whenever the training set has a few thousand examples. For a linear layer $z=Wa+b$, $\partial f_c/\partial W=\delta_c a\T$ with $\delta_c=\partial f_c/\partial z$, so for a multilayer perceptron $G=\sum_\ell(\Delta_\ell\Delta_\ell\T)\odot(A_\ell A_\ell\T+\mathbf{1})$ is assembled from layer inputs $A_\ell$ and output gradients $\Delta_\ell$ in one forward and $C$ backward passes, without forming $J$. The same algebra at a new input $x$ gives the posterior output covariance $J_x\Sigma J_x\T$ and a label-free \emph{prospective volume} $\tfrac12\logdet(I+\Lambda_x^{1/2}J_x\Sigma J_x\T\Lambda_x^{1/2})=h(q_\D)-h(q_{\D\cup\{x\}})$, the capacity $x$ would occupy if it were added to the training set; for a Gaussian likelihood it is the mutual information between the unseen label and the weights.

\paragraph{Networks not trained to a mode.} \Cref{eq:unlearn} assumes $\nabla L(\theta^*)=0$. Networks trained with dropout or early stopping, or analyzed with a prior precision chosen after training, violate this, and a Newton step from $\theta^*$ then credits the residual gradient to whichever example is removed: high-leverage examples that the network did not fit receive enormous spurious memorization. We therefore center the posterior on the MAP of the linearized network $f(x;\theta^*)+J_x(\theta-\theta^*)$ instead. Viewing the linearized network as a Gaussian process with kernel $G/\alpha$ \citep{khan2019,immer2021} and a constant prior mean $m$ (we use the training-set average), this MAP is the posterior mode $\hat f$ at the training inputs, found by Newton's method \citep[Alg.~3.1]{rasmussen2006}. Residuals and curvatures are evaluated at $\hat f$, and \cref{prop:reduction} applies unchanged. For a Gaussian likelihood a single step is exact, unlearning becomes exact Gaussian-process leave-one-out, \cref{prop:bayes} holds exactly, and the Laplace evidence is the Gaussian-process marginal likelihood. Because the kernel $G$ of a trained network has itself been fit to the training labels, maximizing that marginal likelihood over the noise $\sigma$ lets the kernel absorb label noise as signal; we therefore choose $\alpha$ by the marginal likelihood but $\sigma$ by held-out data (\cref{sec:tdc-setup}).

\section{Experiments}
The experiments in this section ran on a single CPU core in double-precision Laplace algebra; details are in \cref{app:details}. The case study of \cref{sec:tdc} is larger and is described in \cref{app:tdc}.

\subsection{Exact check: Bayesian linear regression}\label{sec:exact}
\Cref{app:tutorial} works this setting out from first principles; here we use it to check the implementation. All agree to numerical precision. Per-example degrees of freedom sum to $\tr H=9.091$ effective parameters, and outliers carry a mean of 1.34 bits versus 0.044 bits for inliers.

\subsection{Compression sweep: two moons}
We trained a 2--32--32--2 tanh MLP ($P=1218$) on 300 two-moons points with 32 flipped labels at $\alpha\in\{3,0.2,0.03\}$ (\cref{fig:moons}, \cref{tab:moons}). Weakening the prior increases the effective parameters from 6.8 to 118.5 and moves flipped examples from ignored (1/32 fit) to memorized (31/32 fit). The median capacity of a flipped example rises from 0.078 to 1.59 bits, while clean examples stay between 0.001 and 0.02 bits. Total code length grows from 218 to 451 bits: the decrease in data bits (171$\to$9) is outweighed by the increase in model bits (47$\to$442), so the Laplace evidence prefers the most compressed model, the one that ignores the noise. Only 31--51\% of the total model information is attributable to single examples.

\subsection{A small vision transformer on digits}
We trained a one-block vision transformer (2$\times$2 patches, 16 tokens plus a class token, width 20, two heads; $P=4130$) on 1{,}000 of scikit-learn's $8\times8$ digits \citep{pedregosa2011} with 63 labels flipped to a random other class, at $\alpha=8$ (\cref{fig:vit}). The model reached 0.875 test accuracy and fit 58/63 flipped labels, with $\gamma=847$ effective parameters. Flipped examples cost a median of 3.46 bits versus 0.049 bits for clean ones (71$\times$). Of the 16 highest-capacity images, 14 are flips; the other two are atypical but correctly labeled digits (a ``2'' and an ``8''), which is the long-tail behavior predicted by \citet{feldman2020}. Clean examples follow the $\Inf\approx\Vol^2$ regime (slope two in \cref{fig:vit}b), while flips lie above it. The unique fraction is $u=0.38$.

Removing a whole digit class costs 14--25\% more volume than the sum of its members' single-example volumes ($\rho=1.14$--$1.25$), whereas the flipped set ($\rho=1.07$) and a random clean set of the same size ($\rho=1.02$) are nearly additive (\cref{tab:sets}). Members of a class redundantly encode shared structure; flipped labels and random examples do not. The joint $\Inf$ shows the same ordering more strongly (class ratios 1.8--3.3 versus 1.1 for flips), but it relies on a large joint Newton step and is less reliable than $\Vol$, which depends only on curvature.

\subsection{Validation against retraining}
For 40 MLP examples ($\alpha=0.2$; the 20 highest-capacity and 20 random) and 28 transformer examples (12 highest and 16 random), we retrained without each example by warm-started L-BFGS and measured actual memorization $F_i-\log_2p(y_i\mid x_i,\theta^{\text{retrain}}_{-i})$ (\cref{fig:val}). Capacity ranks actual memorization well: Spearman correlation between $\Inf(\{i\})$ and actual memorization is 0.84 (MLP) and 0.88 (transformer), monotone over four orders of magnitude. The one-step prediction of memorization, $F_i-F_i^{-}$, is accurate for typical examples but not for heavily memorized ones. For all 12 highest-capacity transformer examples it overestimated (about 17--60 predicted versus 6--13 actual bits), and for the MLP it was scattered on both sides of the diagonal (Pearson 0.60 overall, 0.90 for the transformer, the latter driven largely by the separation between groups). This matches reports that influence approximations degrade for highly influential points \citep{basu2021,bae2022}. Computing all 1{,}000 transformer capacities took about 11\,s, less than one warm-started retrain ($\approx$14\,s).

\section{Case study: molecular property prediction}\label{sec:tdc}
Molecular property data sets are small, assembled from assays with limited reproducibility, often pooled across laboratories, and full of activity cliffs, where near-identical structures have very different properties. They are therefore a natural setting for asking which training compounds a model spends capacity on. We applied capfid to dropout MLPs, a standard baseline for these tasks, using the output-space computation of \cref{sec:kernel}.

\subsection{Setup}\label{sec:tdc-setup}
\paragraph{Tasks.} Three Therapeutics Data Commons ADMET benchmark tasks \citep{huang2021} with the benchmark's scaffold split (seed 1): AMES mutagenicity \citep{xu2012}, a binary task compiled from several public sources (5{,}093/728/1{,}457 train/validation/test compounds); Lipophilicity\_AstraZeneca \citep{astrazeneca2016,wu2018}, octanol/water $\log D_{7.4}$ measured in a single laboratory (2{,}940/420/840); and Solubility\_AqSolDB \citep{sorkun2019}, aqueous solubility $\log S$ curated from nine public sources (6{,}986/999/1{,}995; two test SMILES that RDKit could not parse were dropped).

\paragraph{Features.} ECFP4 \citep{rogers2010} (Morgan radius 2, 2{,}048 bits); APDP, atom pairs \citep{carhart1985} together with Sheridan's donor/acceptor binding-property pairs \citep{kearsley1996,sheridan2019}, folded into 2{,}048 bits; and CheMeleon \citep{burns2025}, the 2{,}048-dimensional embedding of a Chemprop message-passing network \citep{heid2024} pretrained to predict Mordred descriptors \citep{moriwaki2018}, standardized on the training set.

\paragraph{Models.} ReLU MLPs with dropout \citep{srivastava2014} after every hidden layer and one output (a logit, or the mean of standardized targets), trained by minibatch Adam with weight decay. For each task and featurization we drew the same 24 configurations at random (width, depth, dropout, weight decay, learning rate; \cref{app:tdc}), trained each with early stopping on the validation metric (AUROC for AMES, MAE otherwise), and kept the best. Models have 0.6--4.2 million parameters. We analyze two training regimes with the selected configuration: \emph{early}, stopped at the selected epoch, and \emph{long}, trained for 100 epochs.

\paragraph{Posterior.} For each trained network we computed the empirical NTK Gram matrix over the training set and the refit posterior of \cref{sec:kernel} with a Bernoulli (AMES) or Gaussian likelihood. The prior precision $\alpha$ maximized the training-set Gaussian-process marginal likelihood (Laplace for AMES). For regression, the noise $\sigma$ maximized the predictive likelihood of the validation split with $\alpha$ profiled out. Maximizing the training marginal likelihood over $\sigma$ as well double-dips, because the tangent kernel was fit to the same labels: it chose $\sigma=0.09$--$0.52$ for $\log D$ and $0.39$--$0.93$ for $\log S$ (versus $0.61$--$0.87$ and $1.00$--$1.39$ from the validation split), and its test predictions were overconfident, with standardized test residuals of standard deviation 1.3--3.7 (versus 0.89--1.00). Each posterior, including hyperparameter selection (a median 80\% of the time), took 35\,s (Lipophilicity) to 7\,min (AMES, AqSolDB) on three CPU threads. We analyzed three training seeds of each early-stopped network and two of each long-trained one; per-compound results use seed 0.

\paragraph{Memorization by retraining.} To test capacity against the networks themselves rather than their linearization, we trained 48 networks per task, featurization and regime on random 70\% subsets of the training set with new seeds, and measured the memorization of each compound as its mean $\log_2$-likelihood under networks that saw it minus networks that did not \citep{feldman2020neural}.

\paragraph{Key compounds.} To pick compounds without hand selection, we ranked training compounds by the percentile of $\Inf(\{i\})$ under each featurization's early-stopped model and took (i) the four with the highest mean percentile (\emph{consensus}); (ii) for each featurization, the compound in its top 1\% whose percentile most exceeds the larger of the other two (\emph{specific}); and (iii) among compounds with below-median information, the one with the highest mean volume percentile (\emph{atypical}). We depict them with their nearest training neighbor by ECFP4 Tanimoto similarity (Tc) and place them in UMAP embeddings \citep{mcinnes2018} of each feature space.

\subsection{Models, posteriors, and validation}
The selected networks reached test AUROC 0.76--0.84 on AMES and MAE 0.55--0.67 $\log D$ and 0.82--1.15 $\log S$ (\cref{tab:tdc}); CheMeleon features were best on all three tasks. The refit posterior mean, which is what capfid analyzes, matched the networks' test metrics to within 0.016 AUROC and 0.03 log units. Posteriors were stable across training seeds: $\alpha$ varied by at most 25\% and $\gamma$ by at most 7\% (coefficient of variation), and per-compound information was rank-correlated between seeds at 0.76--0.95 except for the long-trained AMES networks (0.59--0.86), whose posteriors store almost nothing about most compounds. Rankings were robust to a tenfold weaker prior (Spearman $\ge0.66$ with the selected one) but less so to a tenfold stronger prior (0.32--0.996), which moves compounds from memorized to ignored as in the two-moons sweep.

Per-compound information rank-correlated with memorization measured by retraining at $\rho=0.53$--$0.71$ for AMES, 0.38--0.65 for Lipophilicity and 0.49--0.73 for AqSolDB (\cref{fig:tdc-val}). The one-step estimate from the trained network, which ignores that dropout and early stopping leave $\nabla L(\theta^*)\neq0$, did better only for the three early-stopped AMES networks (trained for 1--5 epochs) and worse for the other 15 models, with values up to 226 bits for single compounds. The magnitudes differ sharply. Summed over compounds, the networks memorized 4 to 400 times more, measured by retraining, than the refit posterior stores (\cref{tab:tdc}). The posterior is a calibrated surrogate whose prior and noise are chosen to predict held-out compounds, whereas the networks interpolate their training labels (train MAE 0.11--0.15 $\log D$ against a validation-selected $\sigma$ of 0.61--0.87). The posterior also conditions on the network's tangent features, so label information that training has already written into those features is not counted. Training AMES networks to 100 epochs shows this most clearly: memorization by retraining grew 4--13-fold, while the posterior's total information fell 5--20-fold and $\gamma$ fell by a third or more, because the 100-epoch features separate the training labels on their own ($L/N$ drops to 0.06--0.14 bits). capfid's ranking of compounds is therefore informative across regimes, but its totals compare models only within a training regime.

\subsection{What the models spend capacity on}
\paragraph{AMES: conflicts with structural alerts.} Every one of the 20 compounds with the highest consensus information is mispredicted by all three networks, and 81\% of the top 100 have a training neighbor with Tc $\ge0.5$ and the opposite label, against 15\% of all training compounds (\cref{fig:tdc-ames}). Most are negatives that carry classical mutagenicity alerts (aryl azides, nitroarenes, aromatic C- and N-nitroso groups, epoxides), and the rest are alert-free positives. The top compound is a diazido-acridine labeled negative whose regioisomer (Tc 0.91) is labeled positive; others are a dinitrophenazine (negative) whose mononitro analogue (Tc 0.88) is positive and prednisone (positive; prednisolone, Tc 0.68, is negative). These are either errors in labels pooled from several sources or real exceptions such as positional effects on metabolic activation; the model cannot tell which, but it isolates a few dozen compounds that an expert can check. Featurization-specific compounds show where a representation, rather than the label, is the problem: compound 5 has no ECFP4 neighbor above Tc 0.26 and costs 1.8 bits under ECFP4 but under 0.01 bits under the other two featurizations.

\paragraph{Lipophilicity: ionization.} The highest-capacity compounds are almost all acids, bases, zwitterions or permanent cations, whose $\log D_{7.4}$ departs from intrinsic lipophilicity by an amount set by their $\mathrm{p}K_a$, which none of the representations encodes directly (\cref{fig:tdc-lipo}). Several are ionization cliffs: a carboxylic acid at $\log D=-0.87$ whose methyl ester (Tc 0.88) is at 2.72, and 2-phenylquinoline-4-carboxylic acid ($-0.70$) next to 2-phenylquinolin-4-ol (2.54). Two labels look wrong: 8-hydroxyquinoline-2-carboxylic acid at 3.30 is unexpectedly high for an acid at pH 7.4, and a phosphonooxy amino acid that is multiply charged at pH 7.4 is recorded at 2.50. The same compounds show the capacity--fidelity taxonomy across models. The APDP network (11 epochs, train MAE 0.49) predicts about 1 log unit for all of them and so ignores them, whereas the ECFP4 and CheMeleon networks (72 epochs, train MAE 0.13--0.15) fit them and memorize them, by up to 27 and 59 bits respectively by retraining.

\paragraph{AqSolDB: inorganic compounds and errors.} Salts, metal compounds and other inorganics are 14\% of the training set but 56--74\% of each model's top 1\% by information (\cref{fig:tdc-aqsol}). Two consensus compounds are recorded as nearly insoluble although both are freely water-soluble salts: sodium 4-hydroxybenzenesulfonate ($\log S=-8.36$) and aluminium sulfate hydrate ($-7.50$).

\paragraph{Representation collisions.} Featurization-specific compounds often have an identical twin: n-hexyl acetate (compound 5 in \cref{fig:tdc-aqsol}) has the same ECFP4 vector as a training compound two log units less soluble. Binary ECFP4 at radius 2 cannot count methylene groups, so homologous series collapse: 16 n-alkanols from C$_6$ to C$_{24}$ share one vector and span 6 log units, and n-alkanes, fatty acids and their esters behave alike. APDP is empty for compounds whose fragments are single atoms and folds polychlorinated biphenyl congeners together. CheMeleon separates nearly all compounds (\cref{tab:collisions}). Per-compound scores understate such groups, because each member is redundant with the rest, so we scored each group jointly with \texttt{set\_capacity}. The 149 AqSolDB compounds with an all-zero APDP vector, from chromium metal ($\log S=-10.0$) to ammonium bromide ($+1.9$), jointly determine 0.72 effective parameters and store 7.2 bits, yet cost 669 bits of data code: the model predicts their mean and ignores them as a set. Members of conflicting groups cost 1.4--3.8 times as many bits per compound as other compounds and account for 32\% of the APDP model's AqSolDB data code. No amount of capacity can fit these labels; only a better representation can.

\subsection{Generalization}
\paragraph{Prospective volume flags uncertain test compounds.} The prospective volume of a test compound, the capacity it would occupy if added to the training set, requires no label. For AMES it rank-correlated with the networks' absolute test error at $\rho=0.57$--$0.67$, and the highest-volume quintile had about three times the error of the lowest (\cref{fig:tdc-gen}). Part of this signal is the predicted probability, since volume grows with the curvature $p(1-p)$. For regression, where volume depends on the input alone, the correlation was weak ($\rho=0.06$--$0.21$): most test error in these tasks comes from label noise and cliffs that the posterior regards as aleatoric, not from compounds far from the training set.

\paragraph{Memorization from longer training did not pay.} Training AMES networks to 100 epochs instead of the validation-selected 1--5 multiplied their memorization by retraining by 4--13 (\cref{tab:tdc}) and drove training AUROC to 1.00, but test AUROC changed by $-0.001$ (ECFP4), $-0.042$ (APDP) and $-0.017$ (CheMeleon). On AqSolDB the CheMeleon network trained to 100 epochs instead of 5 lowered its training MAE from 0.56 to 0.32 and raised its test MAE from 0.83 to 0.89, while memorization by retraining rose from 2{,}487 to 4{,}189 bits. Where the selected epoch count was already near 100 (Lipophilicity ECFP4 and CheMeleon), nothing changed. capfid's per-compound ranking was largely preserved between regimes (Spearman 0.56--0.88), so the compounds memorized by longer training are mostly those that already cost capacity under early stopping.

\paragraph{Description length ranks representations.} Within each training regime, the Laplace code length per compound was lowest for CheMeleon on all three tasks, the featurization with the best test metric (\cref{tab:tdc}). It also ordered ECFP4 and APDP correctly on AMES and Lipophilicity but not on AqSolDB, where APDP's collisions inflate its code length but its test error is lower.

\subsection{Summary}
Across three ADMET tasks, capacity located (i) compounds whose labels contradict their nearest neighbors and well-established structural alerts, including likely errors (AMES); (ii) a physical mechanism, ionization, that the representations do not encode (Lipophilicity); (iii) gross label errors and chemical classes outside the representations' scope (AqSolDB); and (iv) collisions where a representation maps compounds with very different labels to the same vector. It ranked compounds by how strongly networks memorize them, as measured by 864 retrainings, from a single posterior per network, and it showed that networks trained past early stopping memorize far more than a calibrated posterior needs without generalizing better.

An interactive version of these per-compound results ships with the code (see Code availability): it colors each dataset's UMAP embeddings by any of the quantities above, in either training regime, depicts a compound with every model's scores and its nearest neighbors' labels when clicked, and draws the members of each collision group.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\paragraph{A taxonomy.} The capacity--fidelity plane separates (i) \emph{typical} examples (low capacity, high fidelity, held-out $\approx$ fidelity), (ii) \emph{memorized} examples (high capacity, high fidelity, low held-out fidelity), (iii) \emph{ignored} examples (modest capacity, low fidelity), and (iv) \emph{atypical but correct} examples (elevated capacity, high fidelity), the long tail that memorization may legitimately serve \citep{feldman2020}. The prior precision moves examples between (iii) and (ii), and the evidence offers a principled stopping point.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Locality.} The Laplace posterior describes one basin and ignores the implicit regularization of stochastic training; capacities are defined relative to a prior, as any MDL code requires a precision.
\item \emph{One-step unlearning.} Held-out predictions for heavily memorized examples are miscalibrated (up to several-fold); treat capacity as a ranking and held-out fidelity as qualitative in that regime. Joint KL for large sets inherits this problem.
\item \emph{Over-dispersion.} Posterior-averaged likelihoods are pessimistic for confident networks at weak priors; this does not affect $\Vol$, $\Inf$, or $\dof$.
\item \emph{Choosing the prior.} Capacities are relative to $\alpha$ (and $\sigma$). The case study selects them from data, but the Gaussian-process marginal likelihood of a trained network's tangent kernel is optimistic, since the kernel was fit to the same labels; we therefore used held-out data for $\sigma$ (\cref{sec:tdc-setup}). Rankings were stable under a tenfold weaker prior but less so under a tenfold stronger one.
\item \emph{Refitting changes what is measured.} For networks away from a stationary point, the refit posterior describes the linearized network's own MAP rather than $\theta^*$; its totals are calibrated but much smaller than the memorization the networks actually exhibit, and the two agree only in ranking.
\item \emph{Scale and scope.} The output-space computation costs $O((NC)^3)$, so it covers wide networks with up to a few thousand training examples regardless of $P$; the parameter-space route is limited to $P\lesssim10^4$. Neither reaches large training sets with many outputs. Kronecker-factored curvature has made influence functions tractable for language models with tens of billions of parameters \citep{grosse2023}, but whether capacity estimates remain reliable at that scale is untested.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Unique versus shared information.} Leave-one-out quantities measure only \emph{unique} information; \cref{prop:submod} shows they undercount the total, by about half to two-thirds here. A Shapley-style average over removal orders would distribute $\Vol_{\mathrm{tot}}$ exactly and is a natural extension. Tracing each example's marginal volume across nested subsets of increasing size would give an information-theoretic counterpart of individualized data scaling laws \citep{covert2024}. It would also test a hypothesis our class-level results suggest but do not establish: that examples in redundant groups (high $\rho$) are those whose value decays fastest with dataset size, while flipped and atypical examples retain unique information as the dataset grows.

\section{Conclusion}
Defining per-example capacity by Laplace unlearning yields three interpretable quantities (degrees of freedom, volume, and information) that are exact in linear-Gaussian models, cheap to compute for every example, tied to the generalization gap and to the MDL code length, and empirically predictive of which examples a network has memorized. Plotted against fidelity, they give a direct, per-example view of where a model spends its capacity. Computed in output space, they apply to networks with millions of parameters, where, in the molecular property-prediction case study, they recovered contradictory labels, a missing physical mechanism, and representation collisions without being told what to look for.

Capacity is the cost side of a per-example ledger. Value-of-information analyses in economics \citep{abadie2023} and data-valuation and scaling analyses in machine learning \citep{ghorbani2019,covert2024} measure the benefit side: the improvement in downstream decisions or held-out performance that an example buys. The two coincide under a logarithmic utility, where volume is the expected value of an example's label, but not in general. In our experiments, memorizing flipped labels consumed capacity and lengthened the total description of the data, a machine-learning analogue of evidence with negative net value. Measuring costs and benefits on a common scale in bits, and tracking how both scale with dataset size, is a natural next step.

\section{A worked example: capacity in Bayesian linear regression}\label{app:tutorial}

This appendix works \cref{def:capacity} out in the simplest setting that contains all of its ingredients: linear regression with a Gaussian prior and Gaussian noise. There the Laplace posterior of \cref{eq:laplace} is not an approximation, each of the three capacities collapses to a formula in two scalars per example, and each of those formulas turns out to be a familiar object from classical regression diagnostics. The appendix is self-contained, and ends with the experiment of \cref{sec:exact}.

\subsection{A posterior is a cloud of plausible weights}
Write the model as $f(x;w)=\phi(x)\T w$, where $\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^P$ is a fixed feature map --- polynomials, random Fourier features, or the last hidden layer of a trained network. Observations are $y_i=f(x_i;w)+\varepsilon_i$ with independent noise $\varepsilon_i\sim\N(0,\sigma^2)$, and the prior is $w\sim\N(0,\alpha^{-1}I)$: before seeing data we believe each weight is of size roughly $\alpha^{-1/2}$. Stacking the $\phi(x_i)\T$ into $\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times P}$, Bayes' rule gives a Gaussian posterior
\begin{equation}
q_\D=\N(\hat w,\Sigma),\ \ \Sigma^{-1}=A=\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\Phi\T\Phi+\alpha I,\ \ \hat w=\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\Sigma\,\Phi\T y.
\label{eq:tut-post}
\end{equation}
Here $\hat w$ is ridge regression with penalty $\alpha\sigma^2$, and $A$ is the \emph{precision}: large in directions the data pin down. This is exactly \cref{eq:laplace} with $J_i=\phi(x_i)\T$ and $\Lambda_i=\sigma^{-2}$, and nothing has been approximated, because the objective \cref{eq:objective} is exactly quadratic in $w$.

Picture $q_\D$ as an ellipsoidal cloud of the weight vectors that remain plausible (\cref{fig:tut-unlearn}b). The prior is a round cloud of radius $\alpha^{-1/2}$. Every observation squeezes it, and squeezes it along one direction only, namely $\phi(x_i)$. That is the entire mechanism: the three capacities are three ways of measuring one squeeze.

\subsection{Unlearning one observation, and leverage}
To unlearn example $i$ we subtract its contribution to the precision, $A_{-i}=A-\sigma^{-2}\phi_i\phi_i\T$ (\cref{eq:unlearn}), and recentre the cloud on the ridge estimate of the remaining $N-1$ points. The cloud grows, because less data constrains $w$ less, and it moves, because the remaining points prefer a slightly different $w$ (\cref{fig:tut-unlearn}b).

A single number governs the growth. Define the \emph{leverage}
\begin{equation}
h_i=\frac{\phi_i\T\Sigma\,\phi_i}{\sigma^2}\in[0,1),
\label{eq:tut-lev}
\end{equation}
the posterior variance of the model's prediction at $x_i$, in units of the noise variance; it is the scalar $K_{\{i\}}$ of \cref{prop:reduction}. It is also the $i$th diagonal entry of the ridge hat matrix $H=\Phi(\Phi\T\Phi+\alpha\sigma^2I)^{-1}\Phi\T$, which maps labels to fitted values, $\hat f=Hy$. Hence
\begin{equation}
h_i=\frac{\partial \hat f_i}{\partial y_i}:
\end{equation}
leverage is the fraction of its own label that the fitted value echoes back. When $h_i\approx0$ the prediction at $x_i$ is dictated by the other examples; when $h_i\to1$ the model simply repeats $y_i$. Leverage is large where the data are sparse --- the ends of the $x$ range in \cref{fig:tut-fourier}b --- and where $\phi(x_i)$ points somewhere no other example does.

Two classical identities fall out of the same algebra, both exact here:
\begin{align}
y_i-f(x_i;\hat w_{-i})&=\frac{e_i}{1-h_i},\qquad e_i=y_i-\hat f_i,\label{eq:tut-press}\\
\textstyle\sum_i h_i&=\tr H=\gamma.\label{eq:tut-gamma}
\end{align}
\Cref{eq:tut-press} is the PRESS formula: what the model would have predicted had it never seen example $i$ costs nothing beyond knowing $h_i$. \Cref{eq:tut-gamma} is the additivity of \cref{rem:totals} in this special case: the leverages are exact shares of a budget, the effective number of parameters $\gamma=\dof(\D)$, which runs from $0$ under a strong prior to $\min(N,P)$ under a flat one.

\subsection{The three capacities}
Putting $C=1$, $J_i=\phi_i\T$, $\Lambda_i=\sigma^{-2}$ and $r_i=-e_i/\sigma^2$ into \cref{prop:reduction} gives, for a single example,
\begin{align}
\dof(\{i\})&=h_i,\label{eq:tut-dof}\\
\Vol(\{i\})&=-\tfrac12\log_2(1-h_i),\label{eq:tut-vol}\\
\Inf(\{i\})&=\frac{1}{2\ln2}\Big[\underbrace{\frac{e_i^2}{\sigma^2}\,\frac{h_i}{1-h_i}}_{\text{surprise}}\underbrace{-h_i-\log(1-h_i)}_{\text{shape}}\Big].\label{eq:tut-inf}
\end{align}
Everything an example costs this model is therefore a function of just two numbers: where it sits, through $h_i$, and how wrong the fit is there, through $e_i$ (\cref{fig:tut-formulas}).

\paragraph{Degrees of freedom} is the leverage itself, measured in parameters rather than bits. By \cref{eq:tut-gamma} it is example $i$'s exact share of the $\gamma$ parameters the data determine.

\paragraph{Volume} is measured in bits. Unlearning example $i$ is a rank-one change to the precision, so the cloud grows in exactly one direction --- by the factor $(1-h_i)^{-1/2}$ --- and is unchanged in the $P-1$ directions conjugate to $\phi_i$. The ellipsoid's volume therefore grows by that same factor, and $\Vol(\{i\})$ is its base-2 logarithm (\cref{fig:tut-unlearn}b). One bit of volume means the example halves the set of weight vectors that remain plausible. Note what is \emph{not} in \cref{eq:tut-vol}: the label. Volume is settled by where an example sits in feature space, before anyone reads $y_i$. \Cref{cor:mi} says what it then is --- the information the model would gain from \emph{any} plausible label at that location, averaged over them --- which is why we call it the capacity allotted to an input rather than the capacity used.

\paragraph{Information} is the one that reads the label, through the surprise term, which is the squared length of the mean shift $\Delta$ of \cref{fig:tut-unlearn}b in the metric of the unlearned posterior. The shape term is what is left when the label is exactly as predicted, and for small $h$ it is $h^2/(4\ln2)$ while $\Vol$ is $h/(2\ln2)$: a well-fit example has $\Inf\approx\Vol^2$ in nats. That square is the floor in \cref{fig:tut-formulas}b and \cref{fig:tut-fourier}d, the locus of examples that occupy volume but store nothing, and a residual of a couple of $\sigma$ lifts an example orders of magnitude above it.

\subsection{Fidelity, held-out fidelity, and memorization}
Fidelity is just the log-likelihood of the label under the fitted model, $F_i=-e_i^2/(2\sigma^2\ln2)-\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi\sigma^2)$, and by \cref{eq:tut-press} the held-out fidelity $F_i^-$ is the same expression with $e_i$ replaced by $e_i/(1-h_i)$. Their difference,
\begin{equation}
F_i-F_i^-=\frac{e_i^2}{2\sigma^2\ln2}\Big[\frac{1}{(1-h_i)^2}-1\Big],
\end{equation}
is the memorization: the bits of its own label an example loses when the model has not seen it. It needs \emph{both} ingredients. A badly fit example in a crowded region ($e_i$ large, $h_i\approx0$) is simply misfit, and removing it changes nothing; a well-fit example on its own ($h_i$ large, $e_i\approx0$) is predicted by the rest of the data anyway. Only an example that is both isolated and surprising is memorized.

\subsection{The experiment of \texorpdfstring{\cref{sec:exact}}{Section 4.1}}
\Cref{fig:tut-fourier} is that experiment: $N=200$ noisy samples of $\sin2x$, $P=40$ random Fourier features, $\sigma=0.3$, $\alpha=1$, and five targets shifted by $+2$. Panel (b) plots the leverage. It depends on $x$ alone and is largest at the ends of the range, where there are no neighbours on one side; the five outliers are unremarkable in it, because shifting a label does not move the example in feature space. Panel (c) contrasts $\Vol$ and $\Inf$ at the same inputs: $\Vol$ is a smooth curve through $x$, while $\Inf$ scatters over three decades at any given $x$ according to the residual there, and the outliers sit two decades above the rest. Panel (d) is the capacity plane: the bulk lies in a band above the perfect-fit floor set by the noise, and the outliers lie far above it at ordinary volume. The five carry a mean $\Inf$ of $1.34$ bits against $0.044$ bits for the rest (\cref{sec:exact}).

Computing \cref{eq:tut-dof,eq:tut-vol,eq:tut-inf} from $h_i$ and $e_i$ alone and comparing with what \texttt{capfid} returns from the general parameter-space algorithm agrees to $3.2\times10^{-13}$ bits over all 200 examples, and \cref{tab:exact} compares the same quantities against brute-force refits without each point.

\paragraph{What survives beyond this example.} Nothing in \cref{def:capacity} assumed a linear model. For a network, \cref{eq:laplace} replaces the exact posterior by the Gaussian matching the curvature of $L$ at $\theta^*$, the scalar $h_i$ becomes the $C\times C$ matrix $K_{\{i\}}=\Lambda_i^{1/2}J_i\Sigma J_i\T\Lambda_i^{1/2}$, and \cref{eq:tut-dof,eq:tut-vol,eq:tut-inf} become the trace, log-determinant and quadratic form of \cref{prop:reduction}. The interpretations above carry over unchanged. What does not carry over is exactness: \cref{eq:tut-press} becomes a single Newton step rather than an identity, which is accurate for typical examples and optimistic for heavily memorized ones (\cref{fig:val}) --- the main limitation of the method. \Cref{sec:kernel} describes what to do when the network is not at a mode, so that $\nabla L(\theta^*)\ne0$ and even the Newton step misattributes the leftover gradient.

\section{Proofs}\label{app:proofs}
\paragraph{\Cref{prop:reduction}.} Write $H_S=J_S\T\Lambda_SJ_S$ so $A_{-S}=A-H_S$. (i) $\dof(S)=\tr(\Sigma H_S)=\tr(\Lambda_S^{1/2}J_S\Sigma J_S\T\Lambda_S^{1/2})=\tr K_S$ by cyclicity. (ii) With $h(\N(\mu,C))=\tfrac12\logdet(2\pi eC)$, $\Vol(S)=\tfrac12[\logdet A-\logdet A_{-S}]=-\tfrac12\logdet(I_P-\Sigma H_S)=-\tfrac12\logdet(I_{mC}-K_S)$ by Sylvester's identity $\det(I_P-UW)=\det(I_{mC}-WU)$ with $U=\Sigma J_S\T\Lambda_S^{1/2}$, $W=\Lambda_S^{1/2}J_S$. (iii) For Gaussians, $\KL(\N(\mu_0,A^{-1})\|\N(\mu_1,A_{-S}^{-1}))=\tfrac12[\tr(A_{-S}\Sigma)-P+\Delta\T A_{-S}\Delta+\logdet A-\logdet A_{-S}]$ with $\Delta=\mu_1-\mu_0=A_{-S}^{-1}J_S\T r_S$. Here $\tr(A_{-S}\Sigma)-P=-\tr(H_S\Sigma)=-\tr K_S$ and $\Delta\T A_{-S}\Delta=r_S\T J_SA_{-S}^{-1}J_S\T r_S=r_S\T S_{-S}r_S$. (iv) Woodbury gives $A_{-S}^{-1}=\Sigma+\Sigma J_S\T\Lambda_S^{1/2}(I-K_S)^{-1}\Lambda_S^{1/2}J_S\Sigma$; pre- and post-multiplying by $J_S$ and $J_S\T$ yields $S_{-S}$. The linearized held-out logits are $f(x_S)+J_S\Delta=f(x_S)+S_{-S}r_S$. \qed

\paragraph{\Cref{prop:bayes}.} Let $Z_S=\int q_{\DS}(\theta)p(y_S\mid\theta)\,d\theta=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\DS}}p(y_S\mid\theta)$. Then $q_\D=q_{\DS}\,p(y_S\mid\theta)/Z_S$, so $\KL(q_\D\|q_{\DS})=\mathbb{E}_{q_\D}[\log p(y_S\mid\theta)]-\log Z_S$. \qed

\paragraph{\Cref{cor:mi}.} With a Gaussian likelihood and linear model, $q_\D$ has covariance independent of $y_S$. Averaging the KL over $y_S\sim p(\cdot\mid\DS)$ gives the mutual information $I(\theta;y_S\mid\DS)=h(q_{\DS})-h(q_\D)=\Vol(S)$. For a single point in linear regression, $K_{\{i\}}=\sigma^{-2}x_i\T\Sigma x_i=h_{ii}$, the diagonal of the Bayesian ridge hat matrix $X(X\T X+\alpha\sigma^2I)^{-1}X\T$. \qed

\paragraph{\Cref{prop:submod}.} Let $g(T)=\tfrac12\logdet(\alpha I+\sum_{i\in T}H_i)$, which is monotone and submodular in $T$ \citep{krause2008}. Then $\Vol(S)=g(\D)-g(\DS)$. Ordering $S=\{s_1,\dots,s_m\}$ and telescoping, $\Vol(S)=\sum_k[g(\DS\cup\{s_{1..k}\})-g(\DS\cup\{s_{1..k-1}\})]\ge\sum_k[g(\D)-g(\D_{\setminus s_k})]=\sum_k\Vol(\{s_k\})$, since $\DS\cup\{s_{1..k-1}\}\subseteq\D_{\setminus s_k}$. Taking the prior as the base set gives the bound on $\Vol_{\mathrm{tot}}=g(\D)-g(\emptyset)$. \qed

\paragraph{Two-part code.} The Laplace evidence is $\log p(\D)\approx\log p(\D\mid\theta^*)+\log p(\theta^*)+\tfrac P2\log2\pi-\tfrac12\logdet A=\log p(\D\mid\theta^*)-\tfrac\alpha2\|\theta^*\|^2-\tfrac12\logdet(A/\alpha)$, and $\tfrac12\logdet(A/\alpha)=\Vol_{\mathrm{tot}}$. Using $\alpha\tr\Sigma=P-\gamma$ in the Gaussian KL to the prior gives $\KL(q_\D\|\text{prior})=L_{\text{model}}-\gamma/2$.

\section{Experimental details}\label{app:details}
\paragraph{Two moons.} 300 points (\texttt{make\_moons}, noise 0.2, seed 0); 32 labels flipped. MLP 2--32--32--2 with tanh ($P=1218$). Objective \cref{eq:objective} over all parameters; Adam (3{,}000 steps, learning rate $10^{-2}$, cosine schedule) followed by L-BFGS (strong Wolfe line search, history 50, up to 1{,}000 iterations). A preliminary sweep gave 4/32 flips fit at $\alpha=0.3$ and 27/32 at $\alpha=0.1$, so the transition to memorization is sharp; we show $\alpha=0.2$ as an intermediate case. Each $\alpha$ took 4--7\,s including training.

\paragraph{Digits transformer.} scikit-learn digits (1{,}797 images, pixels scaled to $[0,1]$), random split 1{,}000/797; 63 training labels replaced by a uniformly random other class. Architecture: linear patch embedding of 2$\times$2 patches, learned class token and positional embeddings, one pre-LayerNorm block (two heads, width 20, GELU MLP of width 40), final LayerNorm and linear head ($P=4130$). Adam (3{,}000 steps, $3\times10^{-3}$, cosine) then L-BFGS (up to 500 iterations). An initial run with $\alpha=1$ gave 0.83 test accuracy and fit all 63 flips; we report $\alpha=8$. Training took 184\,s; Jacobians, GGN, Cholesky and all per-example quantities about 11\,s; the 12 set capacities about 13\,s.

\paragraph{Laplace and fidelity.} Exact GGN in float64; Jacobians by \texttt{torch.func.vmap(jacrev)}. Posterior expectations used 2{,}000 (MLP) or 1{,}000 (transformer) Monte Carlo samples with common random numbers across the with/without posteriors.

\paragraph{Retraining.} Warm-started from $\theta^*$, L-BFGS for up to 300 iterations on \cref{eq:objective} without the example. Warm starting isolates the local effect that the Laplace approximation targets; retraining from scratch would add basin-to-basin variation.

\paragraph{Hardware and reproduction.} One CPU core, 3\,GB RAM. The full pipeline including validation (\texttt{examples/paper/reproduce\_paper.py} in the \texttt{capfid} repository) ran in about 12 minutes.


\section{Case-study details}\label{app:tdc}
\paragraph{Data and features.} Tasks were loaded with PyTDC~0.4.1 (\texttt{admet\_group}, \texttt{get\_train\_valid\_split(seed=1, split\_type="default")}). ECFP4 used RDKit's Morgan generator (radius 2, 2{,}048 bits). APDP followed the implementation in \texttt{maomlab/MPLearn}: the nonzero keys of RDKit's atom-pair fingerprint and of Sheridan's binding-property-pair fingerprint (the latter offset by $2^{23}$) were folded modulo 2{,}048 into one binary vector. Because $2^{23}$ is a multiple of 2{,}048, both families share the same bits. Atom pairs and binding-property pairs are defined only within a connected fragment, so 149 AqSolDB compounds consisting only of monatomic ions or single atoms have an all-zero APDP vector. CheMeleon embeddings were computed with the published message-passing weights (Zenodo record 15460715) and mean aggregation, as in \texttt{chemeleon\_fingerprint.py}.

\paragraph{Training.} Minibatches of 128, Adam with coupled weight decay, binary cross-entropy or $\tfrac12$MSE on standardized targets. The 24 configurations were drawn without replacement from width $\in\{256,512,1024\}$, depth $\in\{1,2,3\}$ hidden layers, dropout $\in\{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5\}$, weight decay $\in\{10^{-6},10^{-5},10^{-4},10^{-3}\}$ and learning rate $\in\{10^{-4},3\times10^{-4},10^{-3}\}$, each trained for up to 200 epochs with patience 25 on the validation metric. Final models were retrained with the selected configuration for the selected number of epochs (early) or 100 epochs (long), with seeds 0, 1 and 2; test metrics are means over seeds and capacity analyses use seed 0.

\paragraph{Posterior.} The NTK Gram matrices over the training set and between held-out and training compounds were assembled from layer factors (\cref{sec:kernel}) in float64. The prior precision maximized the training-set marginal likelihood of the Gaussian process with kernel $G/\alpha$ and mean $m$: in closed form from one eigendecomposition of $G$ for Gaussian likelihoods \citep[eq.~2.30]{rasmussen2006}, and by the Laplace approximation for AMES \citep[eq.~3.32]{rasmussen2006}, with Newton mode-finding at each candidate; both by bounded scalar search over $\log\alpha$. For regression, $\sigma$ maximized the closed-form predictive log-likelihood of the validation compounds \citep[eqs.~2.25--2.26]{rasmussen2006}, with $\alpha$ re-optimized at each candidate $\sigma$. For comparison we also maximized the training marginal likelihood jointly over $(\alpha,\sigma)$ (Nelder--Mead from four starting points). The prior mean $m$ was 0 for standardized targets and the log-odds of the training positive rate for AMES. Posterior expectations used 1{,}000 Monte Carlo samples for AMES (closed form for Gaussian likelihoods).

\paragraph{Retraining.} For model $k=1,\dots,48$, each training compound was kept independently with probability 0.7 and the network was trained from a new initialization (seed $1000+k$) with the same configuration and number of epochs. Each compound was held out of about 14 networks.

\paragraph{Compute.} All case-study stages ran on a 12-core Apple M2 Max CPU: hyperparameter search (216 runs) about 15 minutes on 10 processes; capacity analysis of 45 networks 143 minutes of CPU time in total (35\,s to 7\,min per network), run as 4 concurrent processes of 3 threads each; 864 retraining runs about 100 minutes on 8 processes. The script \texttt{examples/tdc\_casestudy/run\_casestudy.py} reproduces every number.
